# Australian Payment Rails: The Senior Operator's Briefing

## The map in one paragraph

Australia runs four retail rails: **BECS/Direct Entry** (batch account-to-account — payroll, welfare, bills, direct debits), the **NPP** (real-time A2A, with **PayTo** as its mandate/debit layer), **cards** (Visa/Mastercard/eftpos, regulated by the RBA), and **BPAY** (bill payments). AusPayNet writes the BECS rulebook; **AP+** (the 2021-approved merger of BPAY, eftpos and NPP Australia) operates the NPP, eftpos and BPAY; the **RBA's Payments System Board** sets policy under the Payment Systems (Regulation) Act 1998. Everything below is the plumbing a senior carries in their head.

## BECS / Direct Entry — the workhorse and how it actually works

BECS moved **3.5 billion payments worth $17.4 trillion in 2024 — almost 90% of retail A2A value**. It is a *deferred net batch* system: originators (or their sponsor bank) lodge fixed-width bulk files (the "ABA"/DE file format — its cramped 18-character references literally descend from punch-card limits) and institutions exchange them **six times each business day: 10:00, 13:00, 16:00, 18:30, 20:45 and 22:30 (Sydney time)**. Since **November 2013**, obligations from the first five exchanges settle **same-day** via multilateral runs across Exchange Settlement Accounts in RITS; the late exchange settles next morning at the 9am settlement. No weekends, no public holidays.

The killer operational fact: **there is no positive confirmation**. A direct debit "succeeds" only by *not* dishonouring. Returns flow back with numeric reason codes — the ones you'll see daily are **2 (payment stopped), 3 (account closed), 5 (no account/incorrect account), 6 (refer to customer — effectively insufficient funds)** plus invalid-BSB/deceased/technically-invalid variants — and they typically land the next business day but can take **up to three business days**. So a debit lodged Friday evening isn't truly "good" until mid-week.

Direct debits ride on a **Direct Debit Request (DDR)** — the customer's authority, held by the *business*, not the bank — paired with a **DDR Service Agreement**. Scheme rules (not legislation) require **at least 14 days' written notice** before varying drawing arrangements (amount, date, terms) or unilaterally cancelling. Banks must act on customer stop/cancel instructions, and the customer-claims process is tilted toward the account holder. The **BECS Procedures v E071, effective 1 September 2025**, tightened this materially: businesses must hold **explicit evidence of DDR authorisation including the service agreement T&Cs**, service agreements need **sponsor-bank approval against enhanced minimum standards**, and there are strengthened sanctions/AML screening and account-switching obligations. Utilities, telcos, lenders and subscription businesses carry the compliance load.

**The decommissioning saga**: in **November 2023**, after a three-year consultation, AusPayNet set a **June 2030 target end-date** for BECS. The RBA's PSB classified BECS a "prominent payment system" (**February 2024**) and its **March 2025 Risk Assessment** found transition risks "not sufficiently understood and adequately managed" — no NPP-native answer for direct debits at scale or large bulk files. In **December 2025 AusPayNet removed the June 2030 date entirely**; Chair John Brogden: the majority of BECS members "no longer believe the industry can achieve the target end-date of June 2030." The RBA's **March 2026 Risk Assessment Update** says risks persist — chiefly "insufficient consensus… on how best to process bulk payments" — and warns the RBA "would take further action to achieve outcomes in the public interest" if industry stalls. Current machinery: an **A2A Payments Roundtable** (AusPayNet, AP+, RBA, Treasury, under ACCC authorisation), a **draft A2A vision published April 2026** with a final roadmap due later in 2026, an AP+ consultation on an **NPP bulk-payments solution** (closed 8 April 2026), and a six-monthly AusPayNet reassessment cycle. Direction is settled — BECS dies in favour of the NPP — but the *date* is now officially unknown, realistically well past 2030.

## NPP and PayTo — the replacement, and the adoption gap

The **NPP** (live February 2018) processed **nearly 2 billion real-time payments in 2025** (~155 million/month, ~$7 billion/day) across **128 million reachable accounts** (+12% YoY) and 100+ institutions, with **25+ million PayIDs**. ISO 20022 messaging, 280-character remittance, 24/7/365, settlement in seconds through the RBA's Fast Settlement Service. **Payday Super (from 1 July 2026)** is pushing employers onto it; an ISO message-version and data uplift lands end-2026.

**PayTo** is the productised Mandated Payment Service: a **centrally-stored digital mandate** the customer authorises *inside their own banking app*, against which the business initiates **real-time debits**. Versus BECS DD: account validation up front, instant success/failure (no dishonour limbo), real-time mandate status (paused/amended/cancelled visibility), weekend processing, and amendments that flow through the mandate service rather than 14-day paper notices. It launched **June 2022**; three of the four majors missed go-live and only committed to **April 2023** after the RBA Governor wrote to their CEOs demanding assurances.

**Adoption reality vs hype**: 90%+ of NPP-enabled *personal* accounts are PayTo-eligible, but business-account coverage is still rolling through 2025–26, and industry estimates put **only ~5% of recurring payment volume on PayTo by end-2025**. The **Zepto PayTo Index (November 2025)** shows the curve steepening in pockets: e-commerce PayTo payments **+301% in six months** (~306k payments, **98.9% settlement rate, 4.94s median settlement**), new agreements +120%, account-on-file at ~90% of sector volume — but bank authorisation UX is the choke point (one bank lifted agreement conversion **from 40% to 70%** just by adding real-time push notifications). Translation: PayTo is real, growing, and inevitable as BECS's successor for debits — but the mass migration of Australia's direct-debit books hasn't happened yet, and 2030 completion is aspiration, not schedule.

## Cards — interchange, LCR, and the surcharging ban

The RBA regulates designated schemes (Visa, Mastercard, eftpos) via Standards under the PSRA. The **Review of Merchant Card Payment Costs and Surcharging** concluded **31 March 2026** with the biggest shake-up in 20 years:

- **Surcharging removed** on eftpos, Mastercard and Visa debit, prepaid *and* credit from **1 October 2026** (the RBA lifts its prohibition on no-surcharge rules; networks then ban surcharging). Consumers currently pay ~**$1.6bn of $1.8bn** in annual surcharges; industry implementation ~$25m.
- **Domestic interchange caps cut from 1 October 2026**: debit weighted-average benchmark **8¢ → 6¢**, cap **10¢ → 6¢** (ad valorem **0.20% → 0.12%**); consumer credit benchmark **0.50% → 0.30%**, cap **0.80% → 0.50%**. Commercial credit cap retained. Estimated merchant savings ~**$910m/year**, skewed to small merchants who price near the caps.
- **Foreign-issued card interchange caps and fee-transparency rules from 1 April 2027** (scheme-fee billing transparency, merchant cost comparisons, pass-through monitoring).
- Out of scope for now: mobile wallets, three-party schemes (Amex), BNPL, e-commerce platforms — picked up in the RBA's **Review of Payments System Regulation (issues paper June 2026)**.

**Least-cost routing** (routing dual-network debit via the cheaper of eftpos/Visa/Mastercard): **84% of merchants enabled in-person by December 2025** (from 50% in June 2022); online LCR *available* to 97% but enablement lags. The RBA declined a formal mandate, keeping "expectations" plus six-monthly acquirer reporting. Acquiring economics to hold in your head: merchant service fee = interchange + scheme fees + acquirer margin; small merchants sit on blended single-rate plans; the ban means surchargers must absorb card costs into prices — which sharpens their appetite for cheaper rails.

## The regulatory architecture

- **RBA / Payments System Board** — policy control under the PSRA: designation, standards (interchange, surcharging), access regimes; overseer of the BECS transition.
- **Treasury** — owns the reform agenda (Strategic Plan, June 2023). The **Treasury Laws Amendment (Payments System Modernisation) Bill 2025 received Royal Assent 19 September 2025**, rewriting the PSRA's definitions so "payment system" and "participant" capture digital wallets, BNPL and gateways, and adding a Ministerial national-interest designation power. **Tranche 1 exposure draft (12 March 2026)** brings PSPs into the **AFSL regime** and lets APRA prudentially supervise major **stored-value facilities above $200m** (group aggregate). Tranche 2: safeguarding customer money, unclaimed monies, and a **mandatory ePayments Code**.
- **ASIC** — conduct regulator and licensor (AFSL, ePayments Code). **AUSTRAC** — AML/CTF: PSPs and remitters enrol/register, transaction reporting. **ACCC** — competition; authorised the AP+ merger (**9 September 2021**) with a court-enforceable undertaking to keep eftpos and LCR alive for four years and deliver a QR standard.
- **AusPayNet** — industry self-regulatory body; administers the BECS framework and clearing rules. **AP+** — operates NPP/PayTo/PayID, eftpos, BPAY, ConnectID.

The regulatory throughline: government wants BECS retired onto the NPP, cards cheaper and transparent, and every PSP licensed — but has learned (via the 2030 retreat) that rails move at the speed of the slowest bank's back office.

## Applied to Cadence

This knowledge lets the Cadence pitch operate at the altitude of someone who has run a direct-debit book, which changes four things concretely. (1) **Anchor the pain in BECS mechanics, with numbers**: the demo narrative should walk a real settlement cycle — debit lodged before the 4:00pm exchange, no positive confirmation, code-6 "refer to customer" dishonour landing the next business day (up to three), reconciliation against a punch-card-era ABA file — because 3.5 billion payments and $17.4 trillion a year still run exactly this way, and every ops team in the room lives that lag. Cadence's value (visibility, retry logic, dishonour handling, mandate hygiene) is credible precisely because the rail itself won't fix this soon. (2) **PayTo is the roadmap answer, not the objection**: when a prospect or investor asks "doesn't PayTo kill this?", the senior answer is that PayTo is inevitable but only ~5% of recurring volume had converted by end-2025, business-account coverage is still rolling out, bank authorisation UX is the bottleneck (40%→70% conversion swings on a push notification), and the BECS end-date was formally *removed* in December 2025 — so the realistic world is a decade of dual-rail operation, and Cadence should be pitched as the abstraction layer that runs BECS today and PayTo mandates tomorrow (Mandate Management Service integration on the roadmap, ISO 20022-ready data model). (3) **Regulatory tailwinds are sales ammunition with dates**: the 1 September 2025 BECS Procedures changes mean every debit user must now evidence DDR authorisation with T&Cs and pass sponsor-bank minimum standards — a compliance burden Cadence can absorb; the March 2026 RBA warning of "further action" and the 2026 A2A roadmap keep decommissioning urgency alive without a hard date; Payday Super (1 July 2026) forces corporates onto the NPP anyway. (4) **Know the cost landscape cold for pricing conversations**: from 1 October 2026 surcharging is banned and debit interchange drops to 6c/0.12% — cards get cheaper and merchants can no longer pass fees on, which strengthens the case for low-cost A2A collection (BECS/PayTo) for recurring revenue businesses, and Cadence should quantify per-transaction economics against a post-October-2026 blended card rate, not today's. One caution to carry: the Payments System Modernisation licensing tranche (AFSL for payment functions, exposure draft 12 March 2026) will likely capture payment-initiation/facilitation software — the pitch should have a one-line answer on Cadence's licensing posture, because a sophisticated buyer will ask.
